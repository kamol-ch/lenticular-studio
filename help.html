How the studio works

Lenticular Card Studio is a free, open-source browser tool for printing lenticular cards (3D depth, animation, or both) on an ordinary inkjet printer, with a lenticular lens sheet laid on top. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded. This page explains every number the tool shows, how it is calculated, and what to do when a print does not look right.

How lenticular printing works

A lenticular card is an ordinary print with a clear plastic lens sheet on top. The sheet is a row of tiny, parallel cylindrical lenses (lenticules), about 75 to the inch, ridged on top and flat underneath. The print under it holds several images at once, cut into thin strips. The lens decides which strips you see, so tilting the card or moving your head shows a different image.

1. Each lens shows a thin line of the print

Every lenticule is a small magnifier whose focus lies on its flat back, where the print is. Looking at it from one direction, you see one narrow line of the print under that lens, magnified to fill the lens's whole width. Look from another direction and the focus moves to a different line.

2. The print holds every frame, in strips

The images you want to show are called frames. Each frame is cut into strips, and the strips are interleaved so that every lens covers one strip of each frame, in order. This is interlacing, and it is what Step 6 does. Seen without the lens, the print looks like a scrambled, striped mix of all frames.

3. The angle picks the frame

Because every lens focuses on the same position within its own group of strips, all the lenses show strips of the same frame at once, and your eye reads them together as one whole image. Tilt the card and every lens moves on to the next strip together, so the whole picture changes to the next frame.

The viewing angle is how far you tilt to go across all the strips under a lens, often around 30–45° for a 75 LPI lens. Each frame gets an equal share of it: with 8 frames and 32°, about 4° per frame. Tilt past the end and you start seeing the strips under the neighbouring lens, so the sequence starts again: the picture jumps back to the first frame. That repeat is normal; Step 1 measures where it happens.

4. 3D: each eye gets its own frame

With the ridges vertical, your two eyes look at the card from slightly different directions (about 9° apart at 40 cm), so each eye lands on a different frame. If the frames are views of a scene from slightly different positions, your brain fuses the pair into depth, without glasses. More frames make the depth smoother as you move your head. Step 2 checks that the eyes are at least 1.5 frames apart.

5. Animation and flips: both eyes see the same frame

With the ridges horizontal, your eyes sit side by side along the ridges, so both see the same frame. Tilting the card toward or away from you steps through the frames like a flip book. This suits animation and image flips, where each eye seeing a different frame would only look like ghosting.

6. Why the print has to match the lens exactly

The strip groups must line up with the lenses across the whole card. A 75 LPI lens is 0.339 mm wide, so a 100 mm card has about 295 lenses. If the print's pitch is off by only 0.1 %, the error builds up to about a third of a lens by the far edge, and that part of the card shows a different frame from the rest: you see broad bands or a picture that changes across the card. That is why Step 1 measures the real pitch with a test print instead of trusting the number on the packet.

The best pitch also depends a little on how you look at the card. From a normal distance, the lenses near the edges are seen at an angle, so the print needs to be very slightly coarser than the lens for every lens to aim at your eye. The pitch test measures this directly at your viewing distance, which is why the measured LPI is often a little below the nominal one.

7. Why images ghost

The lens is not a perfect optic, so each eye always picks up a little of the neighbouring strips. Ink also spreads sideways on paper, blurring strips into each other, and a gap between lens and print makes it worse. The fewer printer pixels each strip has, the more this mixes frames. That is why fewer frames, glossy paper, ink-spread compensation and a lens pressed flat all give cleaner results.

Key terms

ppi
Printer pixels per inch in the output file. The default is 720 ppi, native for most Epson inkjets; Canon and HP are usually 600.
LPI
Lenses per inch of the lens sheet (default 75). Nominal LPI is the packet value; measured LPI comes from the pitch test and is used when present.
Pitch
The width of one lens in printer pixels: pitch = ppi ÷ LPI.
Frame
One of the images the lens shows at different angles: a viewpoint for 3D, a moment for animation.
Strip
The slice of one frame printed under one lens. Each lens holds one strip of every frame.
Viewing angle
How far you tilt the card before the frame sequence repeats. Measured in Step 1; 32° is assumed until then.
Ridges
The direction of the lens ribs. Vertical: turn the card left/right, and your two eyes see different frames (needed for 3D). Horizontal: tilt toward/away, and both eyes see the same frame (best for animation).
Ink spread
How far ink creeps sideways into neighbouring light areas on a given paper, per edge, in mm.

Step 1 · Printer & lens

Calibrate once per printer, paper and lens, then export lens-profile.json and import it for every new card.

Numbers

pitch = ppi ÷ LPI

720 ÷ 75 = 9.6 px per lens · 720 ÷ 75.08 = 9.590 px per lens

max frames = floor(pitch), at least 2

Each frame needs at least one printer pixel per lens: 9 frames at 720 ppi / 75 LPI.

Test sheets, in order

Every sheet has a border and a 50 mm bar. If the bar does not measure 50 mm, the print was scaled.

  1. Pitch test → measured LPI. Bands of lines at candidate LPIs. Lay the lens on with its ridges parallel to the lines and look straight down from about 40 cm. Wrong pitches show broad dark and light stripes (beating). The matching band looks evenly toned across its width. A fine thick/thin rhythm on paper is normal (whole-pixel rounding). Plain paper is fine for this one.
  2. Viewing-angle test → measured angle. Eight numbered colours. Tape the lens and sheet to the back of a phone with the ridges side to side, open a level app and tilt toward you. Note the angle where colour 1 first appears and where it appears again: the difference is the viewing angle. Close one eye. Use photo paper.
  3. Ink-spread test → ink spread (optional). A/B bands compensated by 0.00–0.06 mm. Without the lens, the best band shows even, fine stripes with no wave; through the lens it gives the whitest A and the blackest B. Use the paper and driver settings you use for cards.
  4. A/B flip test (optional). A final check with all the measured values applied.

Ink spread does not change the pitch test: it widens every line equally on both sides, so the line centres stay put. Do the pitch test first, because the ink-spread sheet is printed at the measured pitch.

Step 2 · Card & frame plan

Choose the effect (3D, animation, or both), ridge direction, card size, bleed and number of frames.

Frame strip width

strip = pitch ÷ frames px  =  25.4 ÷ LPI ÷ frames mm

8 frames at 9.6 px: 1.2 px = 0.042 mm. 4 frames: 2.4 px = 0.085 mm.

Frames between the eyes (3D)

eye angle = 2 × atan((ipd ÷ 2) ÷ distance)

ipd 63 mm, distance 400 mm: 2 × atan(31.5 ÷ 400) ≈ 9.0°

frames between the eyes = eye angle × frames ÷ viewing angle

9.0° × 8 ÷ 32° ≈ 2.25

Each eye must land on a different frame for 3D. At 1.0 the eyes are one frame apart only at the best spot; at 1.5 or more they stay on different frames as your head moves a little. Below 1.5 the tool warns Weak 3D.

minimum frames for 3D = ceil(1.5 × viewing angle ÷ eye angle)

At 32°: ceil(1.5 × 32 ÷ 9.0) = 6

Depth budget

max shift per frame = pitch × budget px

The budget is in lens widths per frame (0.1–3, default 1). Larger gives stronger 3D but more blur.

Frames for animation

Fewer frames make each one cleaner. With a 75 LPI lens, 3–4 frames is a good start: each strip is wide enough that ink spread and lens blur do not mix neighbouring frames. Use horizontal ridges so both eyes see the same frame.

Working resolution

Frames are stored at about 3 samples per lens across the ridges (the samples per lens setting, 1–8) and at full resolution, up to 720 ppi, along them. A 100 × 150 mm card at 720 ppi / 75 LPI has about 313 lenses across, so a frame is about 940 px wide instead of 3005 px. This saves memory; the print file is still full resolution.

Step 3 · Images

Upload a single scan, or PNG layers with transparency (background, middle, foreground). Drag a layer on the canvas to move it, scroll to resize it, and set its order, opacity and depth in the panel. Layers are what make layer-based 3D and simple animation possible, so separate the parts that should move or float when you can.

Scan watercolours at 600 dpi or more. Very high contrast edges (black on white) show ghosting more than soft watercolour does.

Step 4 · Animation

Simple motion moves a layer or a painted mask with drift, bob, sway, scale, fade in, fade out or flicker, each with easing. Video clip extracts frames between a start and end time, with optional ping-pong (play forward then back) and drift alignment.

Keep the change between neighbouring frames small and gradual. Big jumps between very different images ghost the most. The tool warns Ghosting risk when motion moves further per frame than the depth budget (pitch × budget px).

A painted mask fills the area it uncovers with a soft blur of its surroundings. That works for small moves; large moves need a separate layer.

Step 5 · Depth & 3D

Layer mode

shift = −(depth − key plane) × strength × budget px × (frame − centre)

centre = (frames − 1) ÷ 2. Layer depth runs from −1 (far) to +1 (near). The key plane stays sharp and still.

The tool warns Heavy blur risk when max |depth − key plane| × strength > 1, which means some layer shifts further per frame than the budget.

Depth-map mode

A greyscale depth map (white = near, black = far) warps a single image into each view. Get one with Estimate with AI (Depth Anything V2 Small, downloaded once and run in your browser), build one from your layers, or start flat. Paint with the near, far and smooth brushes, and use edge cleanup to reduce halos. Gaps that open behind near objects are filled by stretching the background.

3D animation from a video clip

With a clip from Step 4, turn on Depth per clip frame. Every extracted frame then gets its own depth map, so the depth follows the subject as it moves: as you turn the card, it both moves and stays in 3D. Estimate all frames with AI runs the depth model on each frame (a few seconds each). The AI judges each frame on its own, so depth can jump slightly from frame to frame; Smooth between frames blends each map with its neighbours to calm that. Pick a frame with Clip frame shown to check or paint its map, or tick Brush all frames to paint parts that do not move into every map at once. Frames without their own map use the single map.

Each frame is both a new moment and a new viewpoint, and your eyes see two neighbouring frames. Keep the motion between frames small, or the moving subject ghosts.

Check the result with the wiggle preview or the red/cyan anaglyph view.

Step 6 · Slice & interlace

Interlacing

For each output pixel column (vertical ridges) or row (horizontal ridges) at position pos:

u     = (pos + 0.5) ÷ pitch + phase offset
phase = u − floor(u)              (0…1 across one lens)
frame = floor(phase × frames)     (reversed if "Reverse frame order" is on)

Because the pitch is fractional (9.6 px is not a whole number), strips come out 4 or 5 px wide in a pattern that repeats every 5 lenses (about 1.7 mm). The average stays exact, so the print never drifts out of line with the lens. Tiled cards and the alignment strips use page coordinates, so one lens sheet lines up with all of them.

Smooth strip edges

On by default. Where a strip boundary falls inside a pixel, that pixel becomes a coverage-weighted mix of the two strips, so every strip carries exactly pitch ÷ frames px instead of rounding to whole pixels.

Ink-spread compensation

shift px = ink spread mm × ppi ÷ 25.4, applied at every strip edge toward the darker strip

0.035 mm at 720 ppi = 0.99 px per edge. With 2 frames the white strip is printed 4.8 + 2 × 0.99 ≈ 6.8 px wide and spreads back to about 4.8 px on paper.

The shift is capped at 0.45 × strip width so no strip disappears. With many frames the strips are narrow, so the cap can be below your measured spread: at 8 frames and 75 LPI it is about 0.019 mm.

Other options

  • Reverse frame order: toggle if 3D looks inside-out.
  • Alignment marks: crop marks plus alignment strips, in which the first frame's strip of every lens is black.
  • Tile cards on the paper: copies share one continuous lens grid.
  • Phase offset: moves the strips under the lenses by a fraction of a lens, if the best view is off-centre.

Print size

The PNG stores its resolution as pixels per metre, a whole number, so 720 ppi reads back as 719.99 ppi. Print at 100 % / actual size, never fit to page.

Step 7 · Print & mount

Printer checklist

  • Glossy photo paper loaded, and the matching paper type selected in the driver.
  • Highest quality mode.
  • Bidirectional / "High Speed" printing off: it misaligns passes and causes banding.
  • Scaling 100 %: fit to page off, borderless expansion off.
  • Auto-enhance and colour correction off (or the same on every print).
  • Print from an app that respects the embedded resolution, such as IrfanView ("original size (from DPI)"), GIMP or Photoshop. Many photo viewers rescale.

Mounting

  1. Let the print dry fully: at least 1 hour for dye inks, overnight is safer.
  2. Find the ridged side by running a fingernail across the lens. Ridges face you, smooth side down.
  3. Lay the lens on the print and line a ridge up with the alignment strip. When aligned, the whole strip goes dark or light at once as you tilt; diagonal bands mean the lens is rotated.
  4. Tape one edge down once it looks right.
  5. Lift the free side, apply adhesive (double-sided film or spray) and roll it down from the taped edge with a brayer to push out air.
  6. Trim along the crop marks with a sharp knife and a steel ruler, through lens and print together.

Privacy and Ask AI

Nothing is uploaded. Images, depth maps and projects stay in your browser (autosaved locally) unless you save a project file. The AI depth estimate downloads a model once and runs it in your browser.

Ask AI opens Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or Perplexity in a new tab with your question, the current step and your settings filled in, and points the assistant to this page. You can preview the exact text first; images are never included. AI answers can be wrong, so check them against this page.
